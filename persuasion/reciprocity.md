# Reciprocity
[Back to this section](README.md) · [Home](../README.md)

## What is it?
Reciprocity is the psychological tendency to return a favor, gift, or act of generosity. In persuasion, it works because people feel an internal pressure to repay what they have received. The principle is often used in marketing, design, and service experiences: when someone gives helpful information, a sample, a discount, or a small benefit first, the audience is more likely to respond positively in return.

This principle is strongly associated with Robert Cialdini, whose work on persuasive influence shows how people are more willing to comply after they have been given something valuable without a direct request. In practical design, reciprocity can create trust and goodwill before a call to action even appears.

## How do you recognize or use it?
Reciprocity appears in free trials, downloadable guides, welcome gifts, content upgrades, small samples, and personalized help. The key is that the benefit feels genuine, useful, and not manipulative. A business is more effective when the value is real and the audience can clearly see that the exchange is fair.

This principle is especially useful in digital experiences. A visitor who receives a useful article, template, checklist, or free tool may feel more inclined to sign up, share the page, or engage with the brand. A small but sincere gesture often builds more trust than a hard sell.

### How to use it in a hero
Place a free resource or helpful offer directly in the hero area: “Get our free planning guide,” “Download the checklist,” or “Try the template.” Pair it with a short explanation of the value so the offer feels generous and relevant instead of pushy.

## Examples
### Free content or resource giveaway
A website offers a free downloadable guide, cheat sheet, or starter template in exchange for an email address. The user receives immediate value, and the brand creates a positive sense of goodwill. That early generosity makes the next step, such as reading more content or buying a product, more likely.

### Free trial or sample
A software company offers a limited free trial or a free version of a product at the beginning of the experience. Because the user gets something useful before committing, they feel more inclined to continue or upgrade later.
