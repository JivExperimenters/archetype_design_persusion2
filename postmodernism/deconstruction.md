# Deconstruction in Graphic Design

[Home](../README.md) · Brennan Dahmen

## What it is

Deconstruction in graphic design questions the idea that a layout merely carries one fixed, neutral message. Fragmentation, competing readings, and tension between words and images make the reader participate in constructing meaning. It is associated with postmodern challenges to modernist clarity, but visual disorder alone does not make a design deconstructive.

Katherine McCoy's Cranbrook poster explicitly sets paired ideas against one another. Ed Fella's Detroit work disrupts polished typographic conventions through hand-assembled, distorted lettering. These are related experiments, not evidence that every designer used one identical method. [Cranbrook Art Museum](https://cranbrookartmuseum.org/artwork/katherine-mccoy-posters-the-graduate-program-in-design/) and [David Versluis's Fella collection record](https://dcaiga.blogspot.com/2010/09/graphic-design-and-postmodernismedward.html) provide the examples.

## Recognize and apply it

| Feature | Effect | Hero use |
|---|---|---|
| Fragmented or interrupted lettering | Makes reading an active process | Break one display word while preserving the full message in supporting copy. |
| Contradictory words or competing layers | Exposes tension rather than hiding it | Contrast two relevant ideas, such as routine and play, around one product. |
| Distortion and visible assembly | Questions the expectation of polished neutrality | Use a rough display treatment above a simple, stable action area. |

This can work for an exhibition, editorial project, or experimental studio. I would avoid using disruption on safety information, terms, or the only explanation of what a button does.

## Historical examples

### Katherine McCoy — The Graduate Program in Design, 1989

- **Creator and work:** Katherine McCoy, *The Graduate Program in Design*, 1989. Offset lithograph, two-sided poster with perforated postcards on the reverse; printed by Signet Printing, Detroit.
- **Institution and source:** [Cranbrook Art Museum, CAM 2008.97](https://cranbrookartmuseum.org/artwork/katherine-mccoy-posters-the-graduate-program-in-design/).

**What I observe:** Opposing words meet near the center, while pink and blue collage fields suggest different kinds of design material. Labels such as image and text form a diagram around the composition. Reading the poster involves relating those terms to the visual objects beneath them.

**Hero adaptation:** I would put two meaningful terms on either side of a product image, then explain the relationship in one plain sentence. That would adapt the conceptual tension without making the offer unreadable.

### Ed Fella — Nu-Bodies, Detroit Focus Gallery, 1987

- **Creator and work:** Edward (Ed) Fella, *Nu-Bodies*, 1987, Detroit Focus Gallery exhibition flyer for Mark Tucker, R. Tim Miller, Linda Kennedy, and Susan Carman. Offset lithograph on warm gray paper; the source identifies an 11 × 17 inch folded, two-sided sheet.
- **Collection and source:** [David Versluis collection, published through DC AIGA](https://dcaiga.blogspot.com/2010/09/graphic-design-and-postmodernismedward.html).

**What I observe:** The title expands and bends as though the lettering were a physical object. Names follow curved paths, and the reverse breaks the title into rotated, compressed pieces. Large empty areas make the scattered marks feel intentional rather than simply crowded.

**Hero adaptation:** I would distort one short title and use its fragments as a surrounding motif. A separate plain-text line would explain the product, with the CTA below it. The title can reward a second look without forcing the reader to decode the action.
